Section One — Foundations of the System

What is Syntōpolis, and how is it different from the utopias of the past?

Syntōpolis is not a utopia in the traditional sense. All previous projects of a new society were technologically premature: they could provide equality only by lowering quality to a common denominator. Syntōpolis appears at the moment when three conditions have coincided for the first time in history: the technology for scaling high-quality education through AI; the infrastructure of global communication, which has reached even the poorest regions of the planet; and the accumulated experience of all previous social experiments, recorded and available for analysis. This is not a dream — it is a response to an objective necessity, with concrete tools for implementation.

Why does humanity need a new system precisely now?

Four factors are operating simultaneously, making the question urgent. The first is a systemic crisis of institutions: trust in governments, media, and international organizations is measurably declining around the world, while global problems — climate, inequality, conflicts — are not being solved by existing tools. The second is a crisis of meaning: statistics on depression, anxiety, and loneliness are breaking records in the richest countries of the world; wealth does not solve the problem of meaning. The third is a physical limit: the planet mathematically cannot withstand all eight billion people consuming like the average resident of a developed country; the model is physically impossible on a global scale. The fourth is a technological shift: AI will eliminate thirty to fifty percent of existing professions within the next twenty years, and existing social systems have no answer to this. Syntōpolis has an answer to all four.

Is Syntōpolis a cult?

A cult is defined not by ideology, but by the structure of exit — more precisely, by its absence. In a cult, a person cannot leave without losing everything: social bonds, identity, and security. Syntōpolis builds the exact opposite: after basic education, every person necessarily goes out into any institution of the external world — capitalism, democracy, socialism, everything that exists — and only after real experience of the alternative makes the decision to return or remain outside. The door is always open in both directions, without any statute of limitations. A society composed of people who have seen something else and consciously chosen this is a fundamentally different structure from a cult.

Does Syntōpolis want to become the only system on the planet?

No — and this is a principled position. The entire evolution of life shows that monoculture is vulnerable, while diversity is resilient. A planet with only one system of social organization is maximally vulnerable: one error in a basic principle — and there is no alternative, no point of comparison, no backup option. Syntōpolis exists as one pole in the competition of systems — open to everyone who consciously wants it. Let capitalism, democracy, and socialism exist. History will judge, as it always has. But for the first time, this competition includes a system built not on fear, not on greed, and not on coercion — but on conscious choice and higher pleasure.

Section Two — The Human Being in the System

What is Syntō, and how does a person enter the society?

Syntō is inner consent. Not a declaration and not a signature under a contract, but a mature, conscious choice to share the values, ethics, and practices of a new society. Entry is through an exam — but the exam merely records what has already happened inside the person; it does not create it. A crucial principle is this: the Syntō of a person who has completed education, gone out into the world, seen alternatives, and returned carries infinitely more weight than the consent of a person who has known nothing else. This is precisely why the mandatory exit into the world is a constitutional principle, not an optional choice.

Does the Syntōpolis education system brainwash children?

Syntōpolis education is built on the exact opposite principle. It rests on four pillars: exact sciences and engineering thinking; embodiment through physical culture; creativity through basic skills in music and visual arts; and history told from different points of view and positioned as an evolutionary search for forms of cooperation. In this system, there is no subject called “the values of Syntōpolis.” Values arise as the natural consequence of broad education — not as imposed beliefs, but as independently drawn conclusions. A person with engineering thinking automatically asks, “How does this work?” and “Where is the evidence?” — and this is immunity against any demagogy, including potential demagogy within Syntōpolis itself.

What happens to a person who disagrees with certain principles of the system?

There are three levels of disagreement. The first is doubt about a specific matter: any citizen may raise a doubt at any time in a review group; this is not merely a right — it is a duty, because silent doubt is more dangerous than expressed doubt. The second is disagreement with a principle: the protocol of philosophical challenge is activated, the thesis is publicly examined by a competent group, and if it is justified, the principle is revised. The third is incompatibility of behavior: a person acts in ways that damage the fabric of society — manipulates, seeks domination, undermines trust. Only here does immunity activate the procedure of exclusion. A crucial principle: behavior is excluded, not thought. The excluded person may at any time take the exam and return if their inner state has changed.

What happens to those who leave and remain outside?

They have made a free choice that the system fully respects. They are not enemies and not traitors. Connections with those who remained inside are preserved. The door is open at any time — after one year, after ten years, after an entire lifetime. Citizens of Syntōpolis who live temporarily outside become living carriers of another model — not missionaries and not propagandists, simply people who relate differently to work, gratitude, and co-belonging. This is the only kind of influence compatible with the principles of Syntōpolis — through example, not persuasion.

What does the conscious release of historical and status-based self-identification mean?

It is not a demand to forget language, cultural memory, personal history, or family traditions. What is released is something else: the conviction that “we are better than they are”; historical grievances as a basis for action today; territorial identity as a source of the right to violence; social status as the definition of human value. Syntōpolis does not ask a person to forget where they come from — it asks them to stop using it as a weapon or a privilege. Physics and biology make this release not a sacrifice, but a discovery: the atoms in your body have passed through several stars; all living people are genetic relatives; the boundary between “I” and “the other” is constructed by the brain as a tool of navigation, not as an ontological reality. A person who has lived through this knowledge discovers that their “I” is already larger than national or status identity. This is not a loss — it is the discovery that you have always been greater than you thought. The historical precedent for this process on a large scale is the United States, where an Irish person, an Italian, a Pole, and a Chinese person became Americans within one generation. Syntōpolis proposes the next level of the same evolution of identity: not a supra-ethnic identity, but a species-level identity.

Section Three — Economy and MASS

How does the MASS system work, and how is it different from money?

MASS is not a means of payment and not compensation for labor. It is crystallized gratitude. Money measures market value — MASS measures the real benefit received by specific people. A street cleaner cleans the shared space — each “thank you” from residents becomes a unit of MASS in their account. This is the birth of feedback, not payment. When the street cleaner wants to go on vacation, everyone who has thanked them can contribute resources so that the trip can happen. The people who contributed resources feel themselves to be part of that vacation — this is not charity, but participation in the fate of a person who has participated in your life. The entire system is transparent: every citizen sees the movement of resources, and public wallets exclude hidden flows.

How does the system recognize the contribution of a scientist who sits in an office and does not communicate with anyone?

In Syntōpolis, there are no isolated people — there are different modes of participation in the organism of society. A scientist in the silence of an office is a cell performing the function of deep concentration for the sake of the organism. AI and the universal information space trace chains of influence: discovery → derivative works → technologies → practices → people whose lives have changed. The system aggregates the gratitude of all who have benefited, and the scientist sees a living map of their influence on the world. This is a fundamentally new kind of higher pleasure — co-belonging through depth rather than through breadth of contact. Loneliness in Syntōpolis changes its nature: it ceases to be alienation and becomes a special mode of participation in the organism.

Why give up private property?

The state assumes the function of ensuring the full life activity of every citizen — housing, food, medicine, education, basic security, and access to communications. When basic needs are systemically guaranteed, private property loses its main function: protection from the fear of scarcity. What remains is limited personal property: personal belongings, items of individual use, and objects of creativity and self-expression. This is not leveling — it is the removal of property as an instrument of domination and as a source of envy, fear of loss, and social hostility. The energy that is now spent on competition for resources is directed toward creation, knowledge, and co-belonging.

Section Four — Education

Why is education central, and not merely one module among equals?

Without education, no other module is possible. Syntō — inner consent — is impossible without understanding what one is consenting to. Hedonism of higher pleasure is unattainable without the ability to distinguish between levels of pleasure. Structuring by competencies is meaningless without a mechanism for forming them. The MASS system requires literacy and awareness. Education is not a module; it is the environment in which all the other modules exist. The metaphor of an operating system is especially precise here: education is not an application; it is the compiler that makes it possible for everything else to run.

How does the education system provide real equality rather than declarative equality?

For the first time in history, AI breaks the link between quality and scarcity in education. Historically, the best teachers were rare; rarity created scarcity; scarcity created a hierarchy of access; and that hierarchy reproduced inequality. Now a brilliant educator creates the core of a course — and AI adapts the form for each student: for a child through metaphors and play, for a teenager through experiments, for an adult through the full apparatus. This is not simplification — it is personalization without loss of depth. The open and free Syntōpolis school is available online to the whole world — not only to citizens. Today, almost every child in every favela has a smartphone. The infrastructural barrier has already been removed by the world itself. The only remaining barrier is the quality of pedagogy. This is precisely what Syntōpolis addresses.

How does continuous education realize free will?

Free will in the real, not declarative, sense equals freedom of choice multiplied by the competence to realize that choice. Present societies declare freedom of professional choice but do not provide the competence for conscious choice. Syntōpolis realizes three levels of real freedom. Freedom of orientation: a unified basic education gives a person a map of the entire space of human activities — one knows what exists before choosing. Freedom of entry: any profession is accessible if competence is present, and competence is attainable through education at any age. Freedom of exit: a person is not bound to a role by fear of loss — basic needs are systemically ensured, so one can change paths without risk to existence. Together, these three levels provide realized free will, as opposed to declarative free will.

Section Five — Evolutionary Foundation

Is there a scientific basis for the idea of humanity as a unified organism?

This is not a metaphor — it is the continuation of a documented evolutionary process. The entire history of life on Earth moves in one direction: from simple isolated units to complex integrated systems. Molecules united into the cell. A bacterium absorbed another bacterium, and together they became a single organism with mitochondria — this symbiosis occurred two billion years ago and is a literal model of Syntōpolis: the absorbed bacterium preserved its own DNA, did not dissolve without a trace, and became part of something larger while preserving its nature. Single cells united into multicellular organisms with a division of functions without a hierarchy of value — a nerve cell is not better than a muscle cell; both are necessary. Organisms united into ecosystems and superorganisms. Trees in a forest are connected by mycorrhizal fungal networks through which they transmit nutrients and warnings — nature invented the internet long before humans and uses it for mutual support rather than competition. The transition to a conscious human superorganism is the next step in a process that has never stopped.

Why is it good for different social systems to coexist and compete?

Because this is exactly how evolution works. Monoculture is vulnerable — biodiversity is resilient. Social systems compete like organisms: the winner is not the strongest in the sense of brute force, but the one that solves the tasks of the current moment best. A plurality of systems is humanity’s evolutionary insurance. With one system, one fundamental error in a basic principle destroys everything. With several systems, the error of one is compensated by others and becomes visible through comparison. Knowledge has become immortal — a defeated system no longer disappears physically; it is preserved as experience and remains available as an alternative. This means that for the first time in history, humanity can allow itself the luxury of conscious choice, rather than only the struggle for existence.

Why are different worldviews a source of conflict, and how does education solve this?

Wars and conflicts are not a manifestation of human malice. They are the result of collisions between incompatible maps of the world, each of which is an incomplete version of reality. People literally live in different realities — not metaphorically, but ontologically. A unified scientific worldview does not cancel cultural diversity — it creates a common foundation beneath it. People may speak different languages, cook different food, celebrate different holidays — and at the same time share the understanding that they are all cells of one organism on one planet in one universe. Syntōpolis education, open and free to the whole world, is capable within one or two generations of doing what no political project has succeeded in doing: creating a common space for the perception of reality as a foundation for dialogue instead of conflict. This is not utopia — it is a question of pedagogy plus technologies that have coincided simultaneously for the first time.

Section Six — Governance and Power

How are decisions made in a system without power?

In Syntōpolis, power as the domination of one person over another is absent. Instead of a vertical of power, there function tools of coordination and process management. Each field is managed by those citizens whose competence in that sphere is confirmed by knowledge, experience, and recognition by the professional community. Functions are distributed not by origin or status, but by the ability to bring maximum benefit to the system. Coordinating functions are not a privilege or a permanent position — roles are reviewed in accordance with results and the needs of society. The objectivity of managerial decisions is ensured by neural network systems that analyze data and reduce subjective distortions. The drive toward domination or the concentration of personal control is regarded as a manifestation of dysfunction.

How does the system distinguish a mature society from a system with a beautiful façade?

A mature society differs from an immature one not by the absence of internal contradictions, but by the quality of the mechanisms through which they are processed. Syntōpolis does not claim that there will be no conflicts, doubts, or disagreements within it. It claims that these processes do not destroy the organism, but nourish its development — just as the immune system of a healthy body does not destroy it, but makes it stronger. The key test is this: can a citizen of Syntōpolis come to the conclusion that the system is wrong about something fundamental — and remain a citizen? Yes — through the protocol of philosophical challenge, where the thesis is publicly examined and, if justified, the principle is revised. This is what makes the system living rather than closed.

Section Seven — For the Skeptic

This sounds beautiful, but human nature is selfish — isn’t it?

Evolutionary biology has long refuted the claim that human nature is exclusively selfish. Kropotkin showed that mutual aid is as fundamental an evolutionary mechanism as competition — perhaps even more fundamental for complex social species. Neuroscience records that acts of cooperation, help, and co-belonging activate the same reward centers as personal achievement. The human being evolved as a social creature — isolation is literally harmful to health at the physiological level. The question is not whether human nature is selfish; the question is which side of human nature the architecture of a system reinforces. The current system reinforces competition and fear. Syntōpolis builds an architecture that reinforces cooperation and co-belonging — relying on the very same human nature.

Why will this not repeat the fate of the Soviet project or other failed utopias?

There are three fundamental differences. First: voluntariness without exceptions. The Soviet project was built through coercion; Syntōpolis is possible only through conscious personal choice. No external pressure can give birth to a society of Syntō. Second: an open door of exit. A Soviet citizen could not leave; a citizen of Syntōpolis can leave at any time without consequences and return if they wish. This structurally excludes the accumulation of internal pressure that destroyed the Soviet system. Third: a scientific foundation instead of an ideological one. The Soviet project was built on a dogma that could not be challenged from within; Syntōpolis is built on a scientific worldview and contains an embedded mechanism for revising principles when justified arguments exist. A system that cannot be challenged from within is a dead system. Syntōpolis lives precisely because it can make mistakes and correct them.
